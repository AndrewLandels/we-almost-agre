The sentence is rude on purpose. That is how a lot of public argument actually arrives: a verdict, a slur, and three different fights taped together.

We Almost Agree does not ask you to like the wording. It asks you to split it.

## The statement

**Electric cars are shit.**

On the site that is a seed map — [open the live split](/claim/electric-cars). You can start from the calmer cousin, “EVs are good.” Same job either way: separate what a reasonable person can already share from what is still in dispute.

## What most people already share

Before anyone puts a point on anything, the map names the overlap:

1. A car has to work in ordinary life — journeys, weather, budget, and the time people actually have.
2. Transport has environmental costs, whether the fuel is petrol, diesel, or electricity.
3. Whether an EV is “good enough” depends on where you live, what you drive for, and whether you can charge near home.

None of that is a dunk. If you dislike EVs because you rent a flat with no charger, you already agree with premise three. If you like EVs and still admit mining and manufacturing are heavy, you already agree with premise two.

The Venn is the point. The first-run map puts the overlap around **64%**. Most of the heat is insult and identity. The live questions are narrower.

## What is still contested

The leftover, on this seed, is three sentences — not a personality:

- For most drivers, an EV is worse than a petrol or diesel car on **total cost** over the years you own it.
- **Public charging** is too thin and unreliable for EVs to replace petrol for people without a driveway.
- Once you count batteries, mining, and the electricity mix, EVs are **not meaningfully cleaner**.

Each of those gets a short trail: typical support, typical challenge, and a line of context. Play-money, when you use it, sits only on these nodes. Shared premises are not a market.

You do not have to accept the demo leans. They exist so the play-money season can settle a couple of claims. Charging is left open on purpose — corridor rapid charging has grown; on-street and rural reliability is still a fair fight.

## If you want to lean in

If the map still leaves you curious — for or against — the useful next step is not a list of companies to buy. It is the **avenues and industries** that actually decide whether those contested sentences come true.

Worth reading around, as education:

- **Charging and the kerb.** Public rapid networks, on-street residential bays, workplace charging, and the unglamorous work of keeping them online.
- **Batteries and materials.** Cells, packs, recycling, and the local harms of mining — the up-front cost in the lifecycle story.
- **The grid.** What the electricity mix does to the “cleaner” claim, and how that changes as generation decarbonises.
- **The used and insurance markets.** Residual values, tyres, and who can actually buy the second car, not the launch brochure.
- **Who the driver is.** A company-car commuter with a driveway is not a Highland cottage and not a London flat. The contested claims fail or hold locally.

That is a reading list, not a portfolio. Nothing here is a recommendation to buy or sell anything.
